# Your first Greek coffee order, in one breath

You sit down at a café in Greece, the waiter arrives, and you have about three seconds. Here is the sentence to have ready:

**"Éna freddo cappuccino métrio, parakaló."**

That is: one iced cappuccino, medium sweet, please. It is the drink half the country seems to be holding on a summer afternoon, and it is a safe first order.

## Why the sweetness comes first

In Greece the sugar usually goes in while your coffee is made. It goes into the little pot for a Greek coffee, and in before the shaking for a freddo or a frappé. So the waiter needs to know now, not later. Three words cover it:

- **skétos**: no sugar
- **métrios**: medium
- **glykós**: sweet

If you say nothing, expect a friendly "*Pos ton thélete?*" (how would you like it?).

## Four drinks, four moods

**Freddo espresso.** Espresso shaken with ice, served cold and slightly foamy. Strong, clean and refreshing. It was first made in Athens in 1991.

**Freddo cappuccino.** The same, with a cap of cold frothed milk on top. Think of it as the friendly version.

**Frappé.** The older classic: instant coffee shaken into a thick foam with water and ice. Its story goes back to the 1957 Thessaloniki International Fair. Ask for it *me gála* (with milk) if you like.

**Greek coffee.** Hot, small and thick, boiled in a little pot and served with the grounds settled at the bottom. Sip slowly and stop before the end.

## Three more sentences

- "Éna freddo espresso skéto, parakaló." (no sugar)
- "Éna frappé glykó me gála, parakaló." (sweet, with milk)
- "Éna ellinikó métrio, parakaló." (medium-sweet Greek coffee)

Do not worry about the word endings. You will be understood, and you will probably get a smile.

## One habit to adopt

Take your time. A coffee in Greece is a reason to sit, talk and watch the street, often for an hour. Nobody will rush you. When you are ready, ask for the bill: "*To logariasmó, parakaló.*"
