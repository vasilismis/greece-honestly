# Why does a Greek P sound like an R?

Because it is not a P. It is **Ρ** (rho), the Greek R. It looks like our P, and that single fact confuses more visitors than anything else on Greek signs. The good news: there are only six letters like this.

## The six look-alikes

| Letter | Looks like | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Ρ | P | r |
| Η | H | i, as in "machine" |
| Χ | X | ch, as in Scottish "loch" |
| Ν | N | n (but the small ν looks like a v) |
| Υ | Y | i |
| Β | B | v |

Say it out loud three times: **Ρ is never p, Η is never h, Β is never b.** Learn these six and the rest of the alphabet gets much easier: eight capitals (Α, Ε, Ζ, Ι, Κ, Μ, Ο, Τ) sound roughly as they look, and ten more are simply new.

## Pairs that make sounds

Greek writes some sounds with two letters: **ΟΥ** is "ou", **ΑΙ** is "e", **ΕΙ** and **ΟΙ** are "i", and **ΜΠ**, **ΝΤ** and **ΓΚ** make b, d and g.

## Try it on a real sign

**ΦΑΡΜΑΚΕΙΟ**: Φ is f, Α is a, Ρ is r (not p), Μ is m, Α is a, Κ is k, ΕΙ is i, Ο is o. *Farmakío*. Pharmacy.

**ΕΞΟΔΟΣ**: Ε, Ξ (x), Ο, Δ (the "th" in "this"), Ο, Σ. *Éxodos*. Exit, and you can see the English "exodus" in it.

**ΛΙΜΑΝΙ** is the port, **ΚΕΝΤΡΟ** the centre (*kéndro*, with the ΝΤ pair) and **ΑΕΡΟΔΡΟΜΙΟ** the airport.

## Why it is worth ten minutes

Signs in Greece are often in capitals. Once you can sound them out, you can find the port, the pharmacy and the exit on your own, match bus and ferry destinations to your ticket, and start spotting English words that came from Greek.
