# The Greek taverna bill, line by line

The food was wonderful, the evening was long, and now the bill is on the table. It looks a little different from home. Here is what each line usually means, so nothing surprises you.

## "Bread"

Bread arrived the moment you sat down, and it is on the bill. That is normal in Greece: bread is usually charged. Next time, if you do not want it, say so politely when it arrives: "*Óchi psomí, efcharistó.*"

## The shared dishes

You probably ordered the Greek way: several plates in the middle, everyone helping themselves. That is why the bill lists dishes rather than "your" meal and "my" meal. With shared plates, splitting evenly is usually simplest.

## The fish

If you had fresh fish, check this line. Fish is often priced **by the kilo**, so the menu price was per kilo, not per plate. The way to avoid a shock is simple and completely normal: before it is cooked, ask for the **weight and the price**, agree, and then let the kitchen cook it.

## The things you did not order

A plate of fruit, a little dessert, a small glass of something? If the waiter said "*kerasméno*", it is **on the house**. It should not be on the bill. Say "*efcharistó*" and enjoy it.

## The receipt and the card machine

You should get a **receipt**, and **card payment** should be possible. If you would rather pay by card, it is fine to mention it early.

## The tip

There is no service line to worry about. Tipping is **optional**. If you were happy, about **5–10% in cash** is a kind gesture, and plenty.

## And one thing before the bill even arrives

You usually have to ask for it. The waiter is not ignoring you. In Greece, bringing the bill before you ask can feel like rushing you out. When you are ready: "*To logariasmó, parakaló.*"

## The two-second checklist

- Bread: usually charged.
- Fish: ask the weight and the price first.
- *Kerasméno*: on the house.
- Receipt and card: you should get both.
- Tip: optional, 5–10% in cash.
