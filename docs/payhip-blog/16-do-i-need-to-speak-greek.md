# Do I need to speak Greek to visit Greece?

No. You can travel around Greece in English, especially in places used to visitors. But a handful of Greek words changes the mood of almost every conversation, and they take an afternoon to learn.

## The five that do most of the work

- **Kaliméra** (good morning) and **kalispéra** (good evening).
- **Yiá sas:** the polite hello, which also works as goodbye.
- **Efcharistó:** thank you. Stress the last syllable: ef-ha-ree-STO.
- **Parakaló:** please, and also "you're welcome". The single most useful word.

## The trap

**Ne** means **yes**, even though it sounds like "no". **Óchi**, with the throaty "ch" of the Scottish "loch", means no. Mixing these up is the classic beginner's mistake.

## When English runs out

- **Den katalavéno:** I don't understand.
- **Miláte angliká?** Do you speak English?
- **Pu íne…?** Where is…?
- **Póso kánei?** How much is it?

Most people will happily switch to English once you have started in Greek, and you will have made a good first impression.

## At the table

**Kalí órexi** is what you hear when food arrives (enjoy your meal). **Yiá mas** is "cheers". And when you are ready to go: **To logariasmó, parakaló**, the bill, please.

## Three with real Greek character

- **Sigá-sigá:** slowly, slowly. Take it easy. You will hear it a lot.
- **Kaló mína:** happy new month, said on the 1st of every month.
- **Kaló taxídi:** have a good trip.

## And one to hope you never need

**Voíthia!** means help. And save **112**, the European emergency number, in your phone.

## How to learn them

Five a day, used straight away. Say *kaliméra* at breakfast and *efcharistó* every time someone helps you. Listen to the rhythm and copy the stress: it matters more than perfect pronunciation.
