# Five sentences for a Greek pharmacy

A headache on the ferry. Sunburn after the first beach day. A sore throat from the air conditioning. Most holiday health problems end at the **pharmacy**, the shop with the **green cross** (*farmakío*). Pharmacists are often a good first stop for minor problems, and many speak some English. These five sentences help when they do not.

**1. "Échete káti giá ponokéfalo, parakaló?"**
Do you have something for a headache, please? Swap the last word: *ponólemo* (sore throat), *pyretó* (fever), *vícha* (cough).

**2. "Ponái edó."**
It hurts here. Point, and you have said most of what is needed.

**3. "Íme alergikós se…" / "Íme alergikí se…"**
I am allergic to… (*alergikós* for a man, *alergikí* for a woman). Say this before anything else is handed to you.

**4. "Póses forés tin iméra?"**
How many times a day? Then: "*Prin í metá to fagitó?*" Before or after food?

**5. "Chriázome giatró."**
I need a doctor. Add "*Miláte angliká?*" (do you speak English?) if you need it.

## When the pharmacy is closed

Pharmacies take turns staying open at night and on Sundays: these are the **on-duty pharmacies**. In Attica, the Pharmaceutical Association lists them online and pharmacies post a notice or QR code in the window. Elsewhere, listing sites show them by town. Schedules can change, so phone ahead if you can.

## One thing to know before you travel

Some medicines sold over the counter in other countries need a prescription in Greece. Bring enough of your regular medication, in its original packaging.

## In an emergency

Call **112**, the European emergency number, or **166** for an ambulance. And if you need people around you to help: "**Voíthia!**"

Save these words in your phone notes today. You will probably never need them, and that is the best outcome.
