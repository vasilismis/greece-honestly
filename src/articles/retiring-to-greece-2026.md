Retiring to Greece is very doable, but the paperwork comes in a particular order. Get the order wrong and you can lose months, or miss a tax deadline that only comes round once a year.

This article walks through the three big pieces: your right to live in Greece, your tax, and your healthcare. It is general information, not legal or tax advice, so treat it as a map rather than the final word.

:::steps
Residence | Work out your route: EU registration, the Financially Independent Person permit or the Golden Visa.
Tax | Decide between the normal scale and the 7% regime, and note the 31 October deadline.
Healthcare | Sort out your S1 or full health insurance, and get your AMKA.
:::

## Step 1: work out which residence route is yours

Your passport decides almost everything here.

### EU, EEA and Swiss citizens

You may live in Greece without a visa. The main formality comes after you arrive:

- After three months, you register in person at the police service for foreigners where you live. In Attica and Thessaloniki that is the Aliens Directorate; elsewhere it is the local police.
- As a retiree you count as economically inactive, so you show that you have sufficient resources and health cover. For health cover, that usually means the S1 form (more on that below).
- The registration certificate does not expire.
- After five years, you can ask for permanent residence.

### Non-EU citizens: the Financially Independent Person permit

Most non-EU retirees use the Financially Independent Person permit. It is designed for people who live on income from outside Greece, such as pensions, rents and dividends.

The income you need to show:

| Household | Minimum monthly income |
|---|---|
| Main applicant | €3,500 |
| Couple (+20% for a spouse) | €4,200 |
| Couple + 1 child (+15% per child) | €4,725 |
| Couple + 2 children | €5,250 |

The process has two stages:

1. **National visa (type D).** Apply in person at the Greek consulate in the country where you legally live. As an example, the Greek consulate in London listed €180 for a national visa in October 2026.
2. **Residence permit.** Once you are in Greece, apply online on the Ministry of Migration platform before your visa runs out, then give your biometrics. The fees are €1,000 for the main applicant, €150 for each adult family member and €16 per residence card.

The permit lasts three years and can be renewed for another three. Apply for renewal in the two months before it expires. Since a 2026 change in the law, a late application is possible up to three months after expiry, but it costs a fine of €100 for every month you are late. Better to put the date in your diary.

:::note Two conditions catch people out
No work in Greece for you or your family members, and full health insurance that covers you in Greece. Travel insurance is not enough.
:::

In detail:

- **No work in Greece.** Neither you nor your family members may take a job or be self-employed in Greece. Foreign pensions, rents and dividends are fine.
- **Proper health insurance.** You need full health insurance that covers you in Greece. Travel insurance is not enough.

### The Golden Visa

If you are buying property anyway, the Golden Visa is another non-EU route. The minimum investment is €800,000 in Attica, Thessaloniki, Mykonos, Santorini and islands with more than 3,100 residents (which includes all of Crete), and €400,000 elsewhere. In both cases it must be one property of at least 120 m². You cannot let it out short term, Airbnb-style. The permit lasts five years, is renewable, has no minimum stay and does not give you the right to work.

## Step 2: decide how you want to be taxed

Once Greece becomes your permanent home or centre of life, or you spend more than 183 days there in any 12-month period, you are normally a Greek tax resident. Greek tax residents are taxed on their worldwide income.

For 2026, pensions and salaries follow this scale:

- 9% up to €10,000
- 20% up to €20,000
- 26% up to €30,000
- 34% up to €40,000
- 39% up to €60,000
- 44% above that

### The 7% flat tax for foreign pensions

Many retirees instead choose the special 7% regime. In short, you pay a flat 7% on all your foreign-source income for up to 15 tax years. To qualify, you receive a foreign pension, you move your tax residence to Greece, you were not a Greek tax resident in five of the previous six years, and you move from a country that has a tax administrative cooperation agreement with Greece.

The difference can be large. On a €40,000 foreign pension, the normal scale comes to about €8,900 before credits. Under the 7% regime, it is €2,800.

The catch is timing. You apply by **31 October** of the year you want the regime to start, and you pay in one instalment by the last working day of December. We cover the details, including who qualifies and what happens if you move after 2 July, in [our article on the 7% flat tax](/articles/greece-7-percent-flat-tax-foreign-pensions/).

One more thing: tax treaties can change who taxes certain pensions. Government-service pensions, for example, are often taxed only by the country that pays them. Check the rules of the country you are leaving, too.

## Step 3: sort out your healthcare

### If you have a UK or EU state pension: the S1 form

The S1 form lets you use the Greek public health system on the basis of your home country's cover. For UK pensioners the steps are:

1. Ask NHS Overseas Healthcare Services for your S1 (+44 191 218 1999).
2. Get an AMKA, the Greek social security number, at a KEP citizens' service office.
3. Register your S1 with EOPYY, the Greek national health provider.

A new S1 arrives every year, and you must register each new one. Easy to forget, so set a reminder.

### What to expect

- Prescriptions usually come with a co-payment of about 25%.
- Treatment in public hospitals is free with a referral.
- The big university hospitals are in Athens, Thessaloniki, Heraklion, Patras, Ioannina, Larissa and Alexandroupoli. Worth knowing if you are choosing where to live.

## A simple order of events

1. Choose your route: EU registration, Financially Independent Person permit or Golden Visa.
2. Line up income evidence and full health insurance (or your S1).
3. Non-EU: get the type D visa from the consulate before you travel.
4. Move, then apply for your permit (non-EU) or register after three months (EU).
5. Get your AMKA and register your S1, if you have one.
6. If you want the 7% regime, apply by 31 October of the year you want it to start.
7. Put renewal and S1 dates in your diary.

Every situation is a bit different, especially for tax. A short session with a Greek accountant before you move is usually money well spent.
