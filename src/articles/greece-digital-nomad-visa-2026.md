Greece has a residence route built for remote workers: the digital nomad permit. If you work for employers or clients outside Greece and want to do that work from Athens, Crete or a quiet island, this is the route to look at.

This article covers the income rule, who qualifies, how to apply in 2026 and the tax points that people often miss. It is general information, not legal or tax advice, and the consulate has the final say on your application.

## The legal basis

The digital nomad permit comes from article 68 of Law 5038/2023. It was then affected by Law 5275/2026, which changed how you apply (see below).

## Who can apply

The core idea is simple: you live in Greece, but your work comes from outside it.

- Your **employer or clients must be outside Greece**.
- You must show enough **net income** (see the next section).

If you plan to work for a Greek company or Greek clients, this is not the right permit, and you should get advice on other options.

## How much you need to earn

The minimum is **€3,500 a month in net income**. For salaried income, "net" means after the tax due in the country where you work.

Family members add to the amount: +20% for a spouse and +15% for each child.

| Household | Minimum monthly net income |
|---|---|
| Just you | €3,500 |
| You + spouse | €4,200 |
| You + spouse + 1 child | €4,725 |
| You + spouse + 2 children | €5,250 |

A few practical tips:

- Gather clear evidence of your income: contracts, payslips, invoices and bank statements, as relevant to you.
- If you are self-employed, your income may vary from month to month. Ask the consulate how they want you to show it.
- Documents in other languages may need translation. Check before you book anything.

## How to apply in 2026: consulate first

This is the biggest change for 2026, so it is worth being clear about it.

Since Law 5275/2026, you must **get a type D national visa at a Greek consulate first**. According to Greek immigration lawyers, arriving in Greece as a tourist and applying afterwards is no longer accepted.

:::steps
Consulate | Check the requirements and gather your documents.
Visa | Get the type D national visa before you travel.
Permit | In Greece, apply for the 2-year permit if you want to stay longer.
:::

So the order is:

1. **Check the requirements with your consulate.** Use the Greek consulate responsible for where you live. Ask for their current document list.
2. **Prepare your documents.** Income evidence, proof that your employer or clients are outside Greece, your passport, and anything else on the consulate's list.
3. **Apply for the type D visa.** The national visa fee set in the law is €75. The consulate will tell you about any other charges. The national visa is valid for up to 12 months.
4. **Travel to Greece** once the visa is issued.
5. **Apply for the residence permit if you want to stay longer.** The digital nomad residence permit lasts **2 years and is renewable**. Your consulate and the Ministry of Migration set out the steps and fees. Keep an eye on your visa's expiry date.

Plan for this to take a while. Do not give up your flat or book a one-way flight before you know your visa is coming.

## Health insurance

You need **full health insurance that covers you in Greece**. Travel insurance is not enough. Check that your policy explicitly covers living in Greece, not just short trips, before you apply.

## Tax: the part people skip

The visa and your tax position are two separate things. Having a digital nomad permit does not, by itself, decide where you pay tax.

### When do you become a Greek tax resident?

Under article 4 of the Greek Income Tax Code, you are generally a Greek tax resident if:

- Greece is your permanent home or the centre of your life, **or**
- you spend more than 183 days in Greece in any 12-month period.

Greek tax residents are taxed on their **worldwide** income. For 2026, salaries are taxed on this scale:

| Income band | Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to €10,000 | 9% |
| €10,000 to €20,000 | 20% |
| €20,000 to €30,000 | 26% |
| €30,000 to €40,000 | 34% |
| €40,000 to €60,000 | 39% |
| Above €60,000 | 44% |

### What about the 50% tax break?

You may have read about a Greek tax relief of 50% for people who move to Greece to work (article 5C of the Income Tax Code). It usually does **not** fit digital nomads. It requires working for a Greek employer or a Greek permanent establishment, and digital nomads by definition work for employers or clients outside Greece.

### Questions to take to an accountant

- Will I become a Greek tax resident, and from when?
- Does my home country keep taxing me, and is there a tax treaty?
- How will my salary or freelance income be reported in Greece?
- What do I need to do when I eventually leave?

An hour with an accountant who knows both countries is worth more than any forum thread.

## The short version

1. You need at least €3,500 a month net, more with a family.
2. Your employer or clients must be outside Greece.
3. Get the type D visa at a Greek consulate **before** you travel; you cannot switch from tourist status.
4. The national visa fee in the law is €75; the visa is valid for up to 12 months, and the residence permit lasts 2 years and is renewable.
5. You need full health insurance covering Greece; travel insurance is not enough.
6. Expect to become a Greek tax resident if you stay more than 183 days, and do not count on the 50% tax break.

If you are thinking about Greece as a long-term base rather than a remote-work stint, the rules for the [Financially Independent Person permit](/articles/retiring-to-greece-2026/) are worth a look too, though that permit does not allow you to work in Greece at all.
