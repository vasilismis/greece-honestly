Nobody plans to need a pharmacy on holiday, but most people do: a headache, a sore throat, sunburn, an upset stomach. Greek pharmacists are often a good first stop for minor problems, and many speak some English. Still, a few Greek words make the conversation easier, especially in a small town or late at night.

The pronunciation below is simplified. Stress the syllable with the accent. "ch" is the throaty sound in the Scottish "loch".

:::facts
pin | Look for | The green cross
chat | Pharmacy | Farmakío
health | Doctor | Giatrós
phone | Emergency | 112 (ambulance: 166)
:::

:::note In an emergency
Call **112**, the European emergency number, or **166** for an ambulance. Shout **"Voíthia!"** (help!) if you need people around you to help.
:::

## Finding a pharmacy

Pharmacies are marked by a **green cross**. The Greek word is **farmakío**.

- **Pu íne to kontinótero farmakío?** Where is the nearest pharmacy?
- **Íne aniktó?** Is it open?

### At night and on Sundays

Pharmacies take turns staying open outside normal hours. These are called **efimerévonta farmakía** (on-duty pharmacies).

- In Attica, the Pharmaceutical Association of Attica lists on-duty pharmacies online, and pharmacies post a notice or QR code in the window.
- Elsewhere, listing sites show on-duty pharmacies by town.
- Duty schedules can change, so phone before you go if you can.

## At the pharmacy

| Greek | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Échete káti giá…?** | Do you have something for…? |
| **ponokéfalo** | a headache |
| **ponólemo** | a sore throat |
| **pyretó** | a fever |
| **vícha** | a cough |
| **éngavma** | sunburn (a burn) |
| **tsimpímata** | (insect) bites |
| **dyspepsía** | indigestion |
| **diárroia** | diarrhoea |

Put them together: **Échete káti giá ponokéfalo, parakaló?** Do you have something for a headache, please?

More useful phrases:

- **Ponái edó.** It hurts here. (Point.)
- **Íme alergikós / alergikí se…** I am allergic to… (*alergikós* for a man, *alergikí* for a woman)
- **Pérno aftó to fármako.** I take this medicine. (Show the packet.)
- **Póses forés tin iméra?** How many times a day?
- **Prin í metá to fagitó?** Before or after food?

:::note Good to know
Some medicines that are sold over the counter in other countries need a prescription in Greece. Bring enough of your regular medication, in its original packaging, and ask a pharmacist or doctor if you are unsure.
:::

## At the doctor

| Greek | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Chriázome giatró.** | I need a doctor. |
| **Íme árrostos / árrosti.** | I am ill. (man / woman) |
| **Ponái to kefáli mou.** | My head hurts. |
| **Ponái to stomáchi mou.** | My stomach hurts. |
| **Ponái to aftí mou.** | My ear hurts. |
| **Ponái i pláti mou.** | My back hurts. |
| **syntagí** | prescription |
| **nosokomío** | hospital |
| **asthenofóro** | ambulance |

Two phrases that always help:

- **Den katalavéno.** I don't understand.
- **Miláte angliká?** Do you speak English?

:::steps
Describe | Point and say *ponái edó* (it hurts here).
Explain | Allergies first: *íme alergikós / alergikí se…*
Check | *Póses forés tin iméra?* How many times a day?
:::

## If you live in Greece

Residents use the public health system through their insurance or a registered S1 form, with an **AMKA**. Prescriptions usually come with a co-payment of about **25%**, and public hospital treatment is free with a referral. See [AMKA explained](/articles/amka-explained/).
