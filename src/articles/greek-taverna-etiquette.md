Eating in a Greek taverna is relaxed. Nobody expects you to know the rules, and nobody will mind if you get something wrong. But a few habits are different from home, and knowing them makes the evening smoother and the bill easier to understand.

This is the etiquette, not the menu. For what to actually order, see [what to order in a Greek taverna](/articles/what-to-order-in-a-greek-taverna/).

## Rule 1: order for the table, not for yourself

The biggest difference from eating out in many countries: **Greeks order several dishes and share them.** Plates go in the middle, everyone helps themselves, and more dishes can follow during the meal.

What this means in practice:

- You do not need to choose "your" starter and "your" main.
- Order a few dishes to begin with. You can always add more.
- If you are a group, agree roughly what you want and let one person order.

Sharing is also the easiest way to try more of the menu.

## Rule 2: the bread is (usually) not free

Soon after you sit down, bread will often arrive at the table without anyone asking. It is usually **charged**, as a small amount per person or per basket on the bill.

This is normal and not a trick. If you do not want bread, say so politely when it arrives: *"Óchi psomí, efcharistó"* (no bread, thank you).

## Rule 3: water

Tap water is common in tavernas, and many bring a jug or bottle to the table. If you would rather have bottled water, ask for it: *"Éna neró, parakaló"* (a water, please).

## Rule 4: fish is priced by the kilo, so ask first

Fresh fish is often priced **by the kilo**, not per plate. The number on the menu is therefore not the price of your dish.

:::steps
Choose | Pick the fish you want.
Ask | Ask its weight and the price before it is cooked.
Agree | Then let the kitchen cook it.
:::

Asking is completely normal in Greece. It is the single best way to avoid a surprise on the bill.

:::note Good to know
Frozen ingredients must be marked on the menu. If it matters to you, especially with seafood, look for the note or ask.
:::

## Rule 5: take your time, and ask for the bill

A taverna meal is not rushed. Plates come when they are ready, and you can stay as long as you like after you finish eating.

The waiter will usually not bring the bill until you ask for it. That is hospitality, not slow service. When you are ready, catch their eye and say **"To logariasmó, parakaló."**

## Rule 6: receipts and paying by card

- **You should get a receipt.** If it does not come, it is fine to ask for one.
- **Card payment should be possible.** If you prefer to pay by card, it is reasonable to ask before you order.

If you are a group, decide in advance whether you will split the bill evenly. With shared plates, that is usually the simplest way.

## Rule 7: something "on the house"

At the end of the meal, you may be brought a dessert, some fruit or a small drink you did not order. If the waiter says **"kerasméno"**, it is **on the house**, a gift from the taverna. Accept it, enjoy it and say thank you: **"Efcharistó!"**

## Rule 8: tipping

Tipping in Greece is **optional**. If you were happy with the meal and the service, about **5–10% in cash** is a kind gesture.

:::quote
Tipping is optional. About 5–10% in cash, if you were happy, is plenty.
:::

## Vegetarians and allergies

Two words help a lot:

- **chortofágos**: vegetarian
- **alergikós** (for a man) / **alergikí** (for a woman): allergic

Even dishes that sound vegetarian can include meat stock or cheese, so ask. With a serious allergy, carry a written note in Greek.

## Quick checklist

- Order several dishes and share.
- Expect bread to be charged; say if you do not want it.
- For fish: ask the weight and price before it is cooked.
- Look for frozen ingredients marked on the menu.
- Ask for the bill when you are ready.
- Expect a receipt and the option to pay by card.
- *Kerasméno* means on the house.
- Tip 5–10% in cash if you want to.

*Kalí órexi!* Enjoy your meal.
