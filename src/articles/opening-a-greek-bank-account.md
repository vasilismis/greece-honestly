A Greek bank account makes daily life much easier: paying rent, setting up utilities, receiving a salary or a pension. The process itself is not hard. The trick is arriving with the right documents, and most of those depend on steps you take first.

This article explains what banks usually ask for and how to prepare. It is general information, not financial or legal advice. Requirements vary from bank to bank, so always ask for the list before you go.

:::facts
id | Identity | Passport or ID card
doc | Tax number | Your AFM
home | Proof of address | A registered lease or a utility bill
id | Non-EU | Your residence permit
:::

## What banks usually ask for

Greek banks commonly ask for:

- your **passport or national ID card**
- your **AFM**, the Greek tax number
- **proof of address** in Greece: your registered lease, a utility bill in your name, or a declaration from the person you live with
- for non-EU citizens, your **residence permit**

Some banks may ask for more, such as proof of income or your AMKA, depending on the account and on your situation. Make sure the address on your documents is current: the bank may send cards and letters there.

## Why the order matters

Most of the documents depend on something else:

:::steps
AFM | Apply through myAADE. You need it for the lease and the bank.
Address | Sign a lease; the landlord declares it to AADE and you accept it online.
Bank | Take your ID, AFM, proof of address and, if non-EU, your permit.
:::

If you try to open an account before you have an AFM and an address, you will probably be asked to come back. See [getting your AFM](/articles/getting-an-afm-greek-tax-number/) and [renting a flat in Greece](/articles/renting-a-flat-in-greece/).

## Your right to a basic account

EU rules (Directive 2014/92/EU, the Payment Accounts Directive) give consumers who are **legally resident in the EU** the right to open a **payment account with basic features**. Under these rules:

- banks offering such accounts must not discriminate against consumers legally resident in the EU because of their nationality or place of residence
- the right also covers people with no fixed address and asylum seekers
- a bank **must refuse** where opening the account would breach anti-money-laundering rules

If a bank turns you down, ask it to explain why in writing, and ask specifically about a **basic payment account**. We have not been able to check how Greek banks apply these rules in practice, so treat this as a starting point, not a guarantee.

:::note Good to know
A basic payment account covers everyday needs such as receiving money and paying by card or transfer. It may not include everything a standard account offers. Ask what is included and what it costs.
:::

## Before your Greek account is open

While you wait, you will need a way to pay:

- keep your **home bank account** open for now, at least until your Greek account works
- check what your home bank charges for card payments and cash withdrawals in euros
- make sure your **home phone number** stays active, as your bank may send security codes to it

## Questions to ask the bank

- Which documents do you need from me, exactly?
- Do the documents need to be originals, copies or translations?
- What does the account cost each month, and what is included?
- How long until I get my card and online banking?
- Can I receive my pension or salary from abroad into this account?

## Common questions

**Can I open a Greek account before I move?**
Some people do, but you will still need an AFM, and banks may ask for a Greek address. Ask the bank directly.

**I am a non-EU citizen still waiting for my permit. Can I open an account?**
Banks usually ask for a residence permit. Ask the bank what it accepts while your application is pending.

**Do I need a Greek phone number?**
It helps a lot. Banks commonly use text messages for security codes. See step 2 in our [first 90 days](/articles/first-90-days-in-greece/) plan.
